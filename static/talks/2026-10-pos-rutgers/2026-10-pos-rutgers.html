Why this talk

This is not a methods talk

It is a tour of three practical problems behind one applied question: how likely is this drug to succeed?

1 · Define the target

What exactly are we predicting? "Success" has to be defined clearly and consistently across programs.

2 · Choose the relevant evidence

Historical benchmarks, Phase-2 data, and expert judgement: how do we choose and utilize the most relevant evidence?

3 · Communicate uncertainty

Decision-makers need an assessment they can trust, probe, and compare, not a black box.

  • Set expectations: the methods are published (Hampson et al. 2022). This talk is about what took most of the effort in practice.
  • Walk the three cards, one sentence each:
    • Target: “success” has to be pinned down like an estimand.
    • Evidence: benchmarks, Phase 2 data and expert judgement, and deciding which is relevant for this program.
    • Communication: decision-makers must be able to trust, probe and compare the number.
  • Transition: first, why anyone invests this much effort in a single number.

Pharmaceutical R&D investment is high risk

Bringing a new drug to market is a highly competitive, low-probability endeavor.

Cumulative likelihood of regulatory approval is:

  • Low: below 10% (Wong et al. 2018)
  • Highly variable across disease areas (Wong et al. 2021)
  • Declining (Chancellor 2024)
  • Cumulative likelihood of approval from Phase 1 is below 10% overall (Wong et al. 2018). The same paper puts oncology near 3.4%.
  • It varies widely by disease area (BIO/Informa 2021 report) and has been falling (Citeline analysis of 2014-2023 data).
  • These come from Biomedtracker, the same source behind our benchmark models (later slide).
  • Message: the base rate is low and moving, so any honest PoS has to start from it.

Failure is costly

Especially late-stage trials represent a substantial sunk cost

Hofmann (2026)

  • Recent headline (Investors’ Chronicle, 15 Sep 2026): a difficult summer of readouts, “the pitfalls of relying on predictions”. Optionally name a recent readout from the article.
  • Late-stage trials are the largest block of development spend and the largest sunk cost when they fail.
  • Predictions drive these investment decisions, so how they are made matters.

When are investment decisions made?

Our most rigorous statistical thinking is applied before Phase 3: when data is richest, and when the required investment is largest.

  • Let the animation play: Phase 1, Phase 2, Phase 3, registration/submission, with tollgate reviews in between.
  • At each tollgate governance decides whether to commit further resources, and PoS is one input.
  • The biggest commitment is the one before the pivotal program: Phase 2b data are the richest so far and the spend still ahead is the largest. That is where we bring out the “big statistical machinery”.
  • Earlier tollgates lean mainly on benchmarks plus structured team adjustments. One sentence only.

Defining the target

What we mean by “success”

Probability of Success (PoS) is the probability that a program ultimately achieves regulatory approval and meets its Target Product Profile (TPP).

Achieve TPP
&
Regulatory approval
→
Success
  • The TPP includes key efficacy outcomes a drug must show to be worth marketing: the estimand for PoS
  • One definition, applied the same way across programs, so numbers are comparable
  • Our mindset: seek truth from hard evidence and scientific rigor, using all available data
  • PoS = P(regulatory approval and meets the TPP).
  • The TPP includes key efficacy outcomes a drug must show to be worth marketing. It plays the role of the estimand: there is nothing to predict until it is fixed.
  • Why TPP and not approval alone: a drug can be approved and still fail commercially or on access.
  • One definition applied the same way across programs makes numbers comparable for portfolio decisions (see backup: who is PoS for).
  • Public “transition rates” or “likelihood of approval” are not PoS: they cannot see the TPP.
  • Mindset: seek truth from hard evidence rather than perceived progress.

A framework for late-stage programs

Our framework entails 4 steps

Developed in 2019 for pivotal-stage PoS (Hampson et al. 2022) and since applied across the portfolio. Program-specific evidence is used wherever it exists: a generic benchmark alone would discard it.

  • Walk the diagram left to right:
    1. Benchmark-calibrated prior.
    2. Bayesian synthesis with pre-pivotal data, then simulation of the Phase 3 program.
    3. Post-pivotal risks: structured scoring of regulatory, safety, quality, technical and TPP risks that the model does not cover.
    4. Manual adjustment, allowed only with a written rationale.
  • Steps 1-2 carry most of the quantitative weight. I focus on them today. Steps 3-4 are in the backup slides.
  • Developed in 2019, published as Hampson et al. (2022), applied across the portfolio since.
  • Design principle: use program-specific evidence wherever it exists. A generic benchmark alone would discard it.

Advocacy for black-box, AI-driven prediction is increasingly loud (Beasley 2026).

  • This is the same problem we are working on, approached from a different direction.

Why not pure AI/ML?

Example of an LLM-driven pipeline (Zhang et al. 2026).

Our stakeholders value a “white box” approach: transparent, traceable, and open to challenge by the people who must act on it.

  • Not anti-ML, and our benchmark models are themselves predictive models (logistic regression). This question comes up in every audience.
  • Example: Xuanwu-TrialMind, an LLM given the protocol and an evidence pack, reports 93% accuracy on 150 temporally held-out Phase 3 trials (Zhang et al. 2026).
  • Our setting is high-stakes and accountable. People must be able to ask “why 5%?” and change an assumption to see what happens.
  • White box: every number traces to a benchmark, a dataset or a documented judgement that can be challenged.

The core idea: Bayesian synthesis

A posterior estimate of treatment effect combines the prior belief with pre-pivotal clinical data

Priorbenchmark-calibrated, before program data
×
Likelihoodpre-pivotal (usually Phase 2b) data
∝
Posteriorbelief about the true program effect
  • For most programs this is the most influential step of the assessment
  • Next: where the prior comes from, then the update itself
  • For non-Bayesians: posterior is proportional to prior times likelihood. The prior is what we believed before this program’s data, the likelihood is what Phase 2b says, the posterior is the updated belief about the true effect.
  • Why a prior matters: Phase 2b trials are small and noisy, and programs that advance are selected on promising results. A skeptical, benchmark-calibrated prior tempers over-reading. As Phase 2 data get larger or clearer, they dominate the prior.
  • The model also allows between-study heterogeneity, so Phase 2 does not translate one-for-one into Phase 3.
  • For most programs this step is the most influential in the assessment.

Where does the prior come from?

Our default stance is often skeptical, driven by industry benchmarks for (often low) success rates of similar programs.

How we derive benchmarks

Data

  • Large cross-industry dataset: >10K programs, US, EU and Japanese markets

Models

  • Three models, one per phase transition
  • Developed internally, validated via time-adjusted cross-validation

Caveats

  • Past programs are not this program: selection, secular trends, transportability

The program benchmark alone does not comprise the assessment. It sets what we believe before looking at the program’s own data.

  • Skeptical by default because base rates are low (earlier slide).
  • Data: curated Biomedtracker extract, more than 10,000 program transitions since 2007. Mainly US/EU/Japan, with approvals skewed to FDA.
  • Models: logistic regression per transition. The three relevant at this stage are Phase 2 to 3, Phase 3 to submission, and submission to approval. Predictors differ by stage. Validated with time-dependent cross-validation and refreshed at least annually.
  • Caveats:
    • No trial-level design information, and no reasons for failure. Transition rates mix efficacy, safety, operational and strategic causes. We convert all-cause failure to efficacy-specific failure before calibrating (roughly 85-99% of failures attributed to efficacy, depending on phase and area).
    • Past programs are not this program: selection, secular trends, transportability.
  • If asked: benchmark is an input, not the assessment.

Benchmark-calibrated prior

👆 Try me (I'm interactive)

The prior is a Gaussian mixture: the weight on optimistic vs. pessimistic components is calibrated to match the benchmark success rates.

  • Open this slide before the talk so the applet is loaded. Use Full screen, Esc to exit.
  • The prior is a two-component normal mixture: one component at the null effect, one at the TPP effect. The weight on the TPP component is how optimistic we are.
  • Calibration: choose the weight so that, pushed through a standard trial sequence (Phase 2b then Phase 3, with stated one-sided alpha and power), the prior-predictive PoS equals the benchmark.
  • Demo: lower the benchmark PoS and click Calibrate Prior to Benchmark; weight moves to the null component. Raise it and the weight moves to the TPP component.
  • Message: an objective, reproducible baseline, not a replacement for program data.

From efficacy to program-level PoS

👆 Try me (I'm interactive)

We simulate the program many times from the efficacy model, combined with a consensus assessment of non-efficacy risks (regulatory, safety, quality, technical), and classify each replicate.

  • Click Initialize Visualization, then step forward through the workflow.
  • Idea: draw a true effect from the posterior, simulate each Phase 3 study (studies, doses, endpoints), apply the success rule, apply the consensus non-efficacy risks (regulatory, safety, quality, technical), and classify each replicate.
  • PoS is the proportion of replicates classified as success (TPP met and approved).
  • Point out: this is not the power of one trial. It handles several studies, doses and endpoints, and the dependence among them.
  • Project teams use the same machinery for sensitivity analysis on design levers (sample size, endpoints, number of trials).

When evidence is indirect: a worked example

Fevipiprant for asthma

Holzhauer et al. (2022)

Phase 3 ongoing, pre-readout. Success rule: significance (1 dose) on both exacerbations & FEV1, in both trials.

Endpoint Quantity of interest Available Phase 2 evidence
Exacerbations Treatment effect on annualised rate of moderate-to-severe exacerbations: the Phase 3 primary endpoint None directly: only a surrogate (reduction in sputum eosinophil counts) in one Phase 2 trial
FEV1 Treatment effect on FEV1: a Phase 3 key secondary endpoint Several Phase 2 studies, but shorter trials in a milder asthma population
  • Neither endpoint’s Phase 2 evidence matches its Phase 3 role: evidence had to be bridged, not carried forward
  • Both treatment effects were elicited, informed by indirect data, and we needed a joint prior for two correlated endpoints
  • Retrospective example: Phase 3 was ongoing and results unknown at the time. Do not reveal the outcome yet.
  • Success rule: significance on both exacerbations and FEV1, in both trials, for at least one dose.
  • Exacerbations (primary): no Phase 2 exacerbation data, only a surrogate (sputum eosinophil reduction) in one Phase 2 trial.
  • FEV1: several Phase 2 studies, but shorter and in a milder population than Phase 3.
  • Neither matches its Phase 3 role, so the evidence had to be bridged, not carried forward. We elicited expert judgement informed by the indirect data, and needed a joint prior because the endpoints are correlated.

Structuring the elicitation

The SHeffield ELicitation Framework (SHELF) protocol (Gosling 2018)

  • Informal elicitation invites well-known sources of bias, so important judgements warrant a formal protocol.
  • SHELF combines individual and group elicitation to produce a single consensus distribution for each quantity of interest (QoI).
1 · Individual judgementsEach expert independently gives a plausible range, median, and tertiles for the QoI.
→
2 · Group discussionExperts share beliefs and rationale; interpretations of the evidence are aired and debated.
→
3 · Group elicitationConsensus judgements about the QoI are elicited and a "consensus" distribution is fitted.

Workshop structure of the basic SHELF protocol.

  • Informal elicitation invites anchoring, overconfidence and dominant voices. SHELF is a structured, facilitator-led protocol.
  • Step 1: each expert independently gives a plausible range, median and tertiles for the quantity of interest.
  • Step 2: experts share rationale and interpretations of the evidence. Disagreement is aired, not averaged away.
  • Step 3: the group judges what a rational impartial observer would believe after hearing everyone, and a distribution is fitted and fed back for checking. It is not an average of the individual views.
  • Next two slides show what the outputs looked like.

Eliciting the marginal: exacerbations

Four experts give range, median, and tertiles independently; and a follow-up facilitated discussion produces a single group judgement.

  • Top panel: each bar is one expert’s judgement (lower plausible limit, lower third, median, upper third, upper plausible limit). Differences between experts are information, not noise.
  • “Pool” is a simple pooling of individual judgements. “Group judgement” is what the group agreed after discussion.
  • Bottom panel: the fitted group distribution, with tertile bands and the lowest/highest 10% tails.
  • Note the x-axis: the judgement is conditional on a 65% reduction in sputum eosinophils. Conditioning on the surrogate is what lets the Phase 2 data flow into the exacerbation prediction.

Eliciting the dependence between endpoints

Repeating the exacerbations elicitation at several assumed eosinophil effects (50-75%) traces out the conditional distributions needed to capture dependence between the two endpoints.

  • Both endpoints must succeed, so dependence matters. If independent, the joint probability would be the product. Positive dependence raises it.
  • Each ridge is the elicited exacerbation distribution at an assumed eosinophil effect (50% to 75%). As the assumed surrogate effect grows, the distribution shifts toward larger reductions and widens.
  • The eosinophil effect is where the real Phase 2 data live, so the data propagate to exacerbations through these conditionals.
  • Details are in Holzhauer et al. (2022). Only go into the copula/concordance approach for FEV1 if asked.

Final prediction of pivotal efficacy

  • Combining the marginals with the elicited dependence yields a single joint prior for both treatment effects, feeding the same simulation machinery as before.

Predictive distribution for each endpoint’s Phase 3 treatment effect, with P(effect beats target).

  • Quantile dotplots: each dot is an equal-probability slice, so probability is read by counting dots beyond the target. Easier for non-statisticians than a density.
  • Read the numbers: P(exacerbation effect beats target) = 34.4%. P(FEV1 effect beats target) = 21.7%.
  • These are probabilities about the true effect, not about trial significance. The trial noise and the both-endpoints, both-trials rule come in through simulation.
  • The joint prior feeds the same simulation machinery as the applet.

What did the PoS say?

  • The resulting PoS reflected genuine skepticism: the TPP did not appear attainable based on the available evidence.
  • Significance, approval & market access5%
  • Approval without market access5%
  • Significance but safety showstopper2%
  • Significance & safe, but not approved0%
  • Insufficient efficacy (no significance)88%
  • Pause and ask the audience what they would have guessed.
  • Overall PoS about 5% (significance, approval and market access).
  • 88% of simulated programs fail on insufficient efficacy. 2% reach significance but have a safety showstopper. 0% are significant and safe but not approved.
  • Another 5% are approved without market access, i.e. would not meet the TPP. This is why defining success via the TPP matters: about 10% approval, but only half of that is real success.
  • The loss is almost all efficacy, so non-efficacy risks were not the story here.
  • A 5% PoS is not “it will fail”. One program is one draw.

What happened?

Elicited prior (top) vs. realized Phase 3 results by dose and study (bottom), for exacerbations (left) and FEV1 (right) (Holzhauer et al. 2022).

  • Top row: the elicited prior from before the readout, with the TPP as a dashed line. Bottom row: realized Phase 3 estimates with intervals, by dose and study, plus pooled. Exacerbations on the left, FEV1 on the right.
  • Results landed inside the bulk of the elicited distributions and well short of the TPP on both endpoints. Judgement before the readout was well aligned with outcome (Holzhauer et al. 2022).
  • Caveat: one program does not validate the approach. PoS can only be checked in aggregate. The value was a skeptical, documented assessment made before the data were in.

Reflections

Takeaways

  • Framing is most of the work. What is the target, and which evidence is relevant to it, matter more than the choice of model.
  • Embrace uncertainty explicitly. Priors, bridging assumptions, and expert judgement all go on the table, in documented form.
  • Transparency is paramount when people must act on the number and defend it.
  • Drug development is hard and pivotal failures are impactful. Rigor and objectivity are what make PoS predictions reliable.

Limitations and open questions

Limitations

  • Benchmarks describe past programs, not this one
  • Elicitation is structured, but remains judgement
  • A single program is a one-off event: PoS can only be checked in aggregate

Open questions

  • Improvements on the concept of calibrated benchmark priors
  • Bridging models from surrogates to clinical endpoints, and across populations
  • Effective visual communication of uncertainty to decision-makers
  • Be candid on limitations: benchmarks describe past programs, and falling success rates mean the past may not predict the future. Elicitation is structured but still judgement. A single program is a one-off event.
  • Likely question: how do you know PoS is calibrated? Calibration can only be assessed in aggregate across programs. Answer with what has actually been evaluated.
  • Open questions, invite ideas from the audience (good student projects):
    • Better calibrated benchmark priors, e.g. more granular program characteristics and other modelling strategies.
    • Bridging models from surrogates to clinical endpoints, and across populations.

Who is PoS for?

PoS serves wide-ranging stakeholders across the company

Stakeholder Decisions What they need from PoS
Portfolio / Finance Valuation, prioritisation, resource allocation Comparability across assets; a consistent, benchmarkable definition
Governance Tollgate decisions A defensible, reproducible, auditable assessment
Leadership Risk evaluation and communication, go/no-go Where the risk sits, and how it changes with new evidence
Project teams Design decisions: sample size, endpoints, TPP, number of trials Sensitivity of PoS to design levers; the value of more data
  • Use if asked who this is for.
  • Four groups: portfolio/finance, governance, leadership, project teams. The common thread is a consistent, defensible, reproducible number.
  • Project teams use sensitivity of PoS to design levers (sample size, endpoints, TPP, number of trials) and the value of more data.

Step 3 · Post-pivotal risks

Conditional on a positive pivotal program on the key endpoints, with no major safety showstopper, what is the remaining probability of ultimate success?

Five factors are each scored via consensus among the project team

Risk factor Low Medium High
Alignment with key regulator Clear agreement or strong positive feedback Generally positive feedback, but unresolved issues Inadequate discussion or important disagreement
Unaccounted safety risks No known unaccounted safety risks Some known risks, but limited relevance to the program Significant risks likely to apply to the program
Quality & compliance risks Low expected risk Medium expected risk High expected risk
Technical development risks No major known technical risks; pre- and post-pivotal products very similar Some meaningful technical risks or product differences Multiple or significant technical risks
Unaccounted TPP risks Low risk of missing important additional TPP features Medium risk High risk, even assuming key pivotal endpoints are met
  • Question: given positive pivotal results on the key endpoints and no safety showstopper, what is the remaining probability of ultimate success?
  • Five factors, each scored low/medium/high (A/B/C) by project-team consensus.
  • The scores map to a probability through a logistic model fitted to an expert elicitation of risk profiles: intercept 2.68 (all-low is about 94%), additive penalties on the logit scale for medium and high.
  • Largest high-risk penalties: unaccounted TPP risks (-2.11), then technical and unaccounted safety risks (about -1.98). An all-medium profile is about 24%.
  • Final PoS = core pivotal PoS times this conditional probability.
  • Benefit is transparency: teams can see which risks were invoked and how they translate into the number.

Step 4 · Manual adjustment

To account for any information not captured in Steps 1-3, teams may override the computed PoS with a written, documented, agreed-upon rationale

Legitimate uses

  • Safety risk already discharged by a large program in another indication
  • Regulatory precedent the benchmark cannot see
  • Known bridging issues between pre-pivotal and pivotal populations

Not legitimate

  • "The number looks low"
  • Undocumented optimism
  • Re-litigating the TPP after the fact
  • Overrides are allowed only with a written, documented, agreed rationale.
  • Legitimate: information the model cannot see (safety discharged elsewhere, regulatory precedent, known bridging issues).
  • Not legitimate: “the number looks low”, undocumented optimism, re-litigating the TPP afterwards.
  • Documentation makes overrides auditable, so we can learn whether they help.

Fevipiprant: what direct Phase 2 data existed?

Direct Phase 2 trial data exists only for the surrogate: sputum eosinophils

A standard Bayesian update for the surrogate: prior 24% → posterior 80% probability that the effect exceeds the target.

  • Direct Phase 2 data exist only for the surrogate, sputum eosinophils.
  • A standard Bayesian update moves the probability that the effect exceeds the target from 24% (prior) to 80% (posterior).
  • Example input (example/asthma.json): estimate -0.54 on the log scale (95% CI -0.84 to -0.24) against a target of -0.30.
